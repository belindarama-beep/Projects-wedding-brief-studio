01 / FoundationPage 02
The document proves the understanding happened.
The couple's real desire was never a beautiful document — it's the feeling of being understood. The document is just the evidence that understanding took place. The planner is who delivers that feeling and gets the professional credit for it. Every design decision in this system exists to make that proof land, with a fraction of the effort it currently takes to produce.
That means the brand has to hold two registers at once: warm enough to feel personal to a couple, disciplined enough to feel dependable to a planner running several weddings at once.
Who it's for
Independent Australian wedding planners — capable, already using Canva or client decks, losing time to interpretation and rebuilding polish from scratch.
Who it's not for
Large, design-led luxury studios who treat creative interpretation as their own proprietary skill — not a problem they want solved for them.
not a design tool. a trust machine.

02 / The MarkPage 03
One arch, used everywhere — never the studio's name on a planner's document.
The curved arch is the one recurring shape across the brand: the panel edge on every document page, the dome of a palette swatch, the card shape in the app. It's what makes a Terracotta document and a Fig document still recognisably the same studio.
But the printed and exported documents go out under the planner's own business name, not "Wedding Brief Studio." The studio's identity lives in the app chrome only. Every document needs a planner mark slot — logo image if supplied, gracefully falling back to a styled text wordmark if not.

03 / Colour SystemPage 04
One dark anchor. One softened counterpart. One accent that gives it teeth.
Four fixed presets, not an open-ended design system. Every document is one structural layout, skinned by whichever preset a project resolves to. The interest comes from dark/light contrast, not any single colour — every preset here carries a genuine near-black or near-plum anchor, checked against a real reference image rather than trusted from an automated colour extraction. 03 / Colour System — cont.Page 05
Where presets are chosen, and by whom.
  • The preset is a planner-controlled, project-level setting — chosen once per wedding in the app, not fiddled with per document.
  • The couple only ever sees the one resolved palette. There is no couple-facing switcher, on any document or screen — the choice is the planner's craft judgement, not the client's toggle.
  • Changing the preset regenerates the Creative Direction and Supplier Brief together, so the two documents never drift apart in palette.
  • A fifth preset is a matter of assigning new hex values to the same handful of tokens — but only once a genuine dark anchor has been confirmed against the real source image, not assumed from an automated colour extraction. Two of the original presets were cut specifically for skipping this check.
Do

Let the eyebrow, callout border and hand-note all pull from one dark token per preset, so a swap re-skins the whole page at once.

Don't

Introduce a colour that isn't one of this preset's named token values, even for "just this one section."

the planner's call, not the couple's toggle.

04 / TypographyPage 06
Structure speaks in Mono. Warmth speaks in Sans. The signature speaks once, by hand.
IBM Plex Mono — structure & labels
EYEBROWS · HEADLINES · FOOTERS
Every eyebrow, page number, section label and headline runs in Plex Mono. It's what gives the documents their considered, almost architectural feel against the softness of the colour system.
IBM Plex Sans — body copy
The reading experience — direction narratives, supplier notes, open questions.
Everything a couple or supplier actually sits and reads runs in Plex Sans. Clean and humanist, never competing with the structural Mono around it.
Beau Rivage — the handwritten accent
feels like them, not like anyone else.
Open-licensed, genuinely calligraphic — not a casual marker font. Used sparingly: once per page, maximum, as a recurring signature device rather than decoration. If it shows up twice on a page, it's stopped meaning anything.

05 / Layout PrinciplesPage 07
Interrupted design — offbeat on purpose, not polished into anonymity.
The working name for the layout language: less standard AI-luxury, more intentionally asymmetric. Elements get pushed to the edges. Text boxes carry the emphasis, not centred hero type.
  • Left-aligned, asymmetric — never centred compositions. The eye should land somewhere specific, not in the middle.
  • Arch-edged panel, not a hard rectangle — every dark colour block echoes the brand's curved arch shape.
  • Depth through layering, not through square footage — presence comes from a stack of shapes with real shadow between them, contained to a defined zone. Never a full-bleed dark background standing in for drama; that's expensive to print and it isn't what's actually doing the work.
  • Hairline borders throughout — thin enough to organise, never heavy enough to feel like a form.
  • Rounded callout boxes with a slight rotation — a degree or two off true, so nothing reads as machine-generated.
  • One handwritten marginal note per page — the Beau Rivage signature device, never more than once.
Do

Let a callout sit slightly off-grid and rotated. Push the hand-note into a margin, not a centred caption.

Don't

Centre a headline "for balance," or square off a panel edge to make it easier to lay out.

07 / Voice, Tone & Sign-offPage 09
Speak like the planner, not like the software.
Every document is written as if the planner is speaking directly to the couple or supplier — warm, plain-language, specific to this wedding. Contradictions get surfaced honestly ("relaxed" sitting against "very luxurious") rather than smoothed into generic safety. A couple should feel read, not processed.
Do

Name the open question plainly: "We haven't landed on the linen colour yet."

Write budget tiers as real trade-offs, not upsell copy.

Don't

Paper over a genuine contradiction with vague, safe language.

Let the tone drift toward generic "luxury AI" copywriting.
